## Frame

A delta stunt kite is a triangle held open by four spars: a spine down the centre, two leading-edge spars along the front edges, and a spreader that pushes the leading edges apart. Carbon tube is the usual choice for all four because it is stiff for its weight and springs back from the shocks a stunt kite takes on landing. Fibreglass is cheaper and heavier; it is a fair choice for a first frame that is expected to break.

The spreader is the tuning spar. A longer spreader flattens the kite and makes it faster and less stable; a shorter one deepens the dihedral and steadies it. Most builders cut the spreader slightly long and trim after the first flight, once the bridle has been set (see [[bridle-tuning]]).

> [!tip] Cut the spine last
> The spine length fixes the nose angle once the leading edges are joined. Cutting it first, to a number from a plan, is the most common way to end up with a kite that will not sit square.

## Sail

The sail is ripstop nylon, cut as two mirrored panels joined along the spine. Construction order matters: hem the trailing edge first while the panel is flat, then join the panels along the spine seam, then sew the leading-edge sleeves that the spars slide into. Reinforce the nose with a folded patch before fitting any spar; the nose takes every crash.

Bridle attachment points are marked on the sail at the spine and at each leading edge before the sleeves are closed. Their positions come from the tuning guide, not from the sail pattern.

## Line

Two flying lines, equal length to within a centimetre. For a 1.8 m sail, 2 mm braided polyester line at 30 m is the common starting point; 2.5 mm adds drag but survives more abuse. The demo project ([[Projects/example-project/README|example-project]]) has not chosen between them yet.
