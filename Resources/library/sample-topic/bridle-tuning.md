## What the bridle sets

The bridle is the short lines that join the flying lines to the kite. Where they meet is the tow point, and its position along the spine decides the angle the kite holds against the wind. A tow point moved toward the nose pitches the kite forward: more speed, more pull, and a tendency to overfly and stall at the top of the window. Moved toward the tail, the kite sits back: slower, steadier, and prone to sliding backward in light wind.

## Starting position

For a 1.8 m delta built as described in [[kite-building]], the starting tow point sits about one third of the spine length from the nose. Mark it before the first flight. Both sides of the bridle must match to the millimetre; an uneven bridle shows up as a kite that turns one way faster than the other, which is easy to blame on the pilot.

> [!warning] Adjust one thing per flight
> Change the tow point, or the spreader length, never both between flights. Two changes at once cannot be attributed, and the second flight teaches nothing.

## Adjustment sequence

1. Fly in steady 8 to 15 km/h wind. Note whether the kite stalls at the top of the window (tow point too far forward) or slides back in lulls (too far back).
2. Move the tow point 5 mm in the corrective direction. Re-check that both sides match.
3. Repeat until the kite holds the top of the window without stalling. Then, and only then, consider the spreader.
